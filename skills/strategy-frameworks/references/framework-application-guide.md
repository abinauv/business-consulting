# Framework Application Guide

Step-by-step instructions for applying each major strategy framework in a consulting engagement. For each framework: preparation, data collection, analysis, presentation, and common mistakes.

---

## 1. SWOT / TOWS Analysis

### Preparation
- Define the scope: Are you analyzing the entire company, a business unit, or a specific product line? Narrow scope produces sharper insights.
- Identify the strategic question driving the analysis (e.g., "Should we enter market X?" or "How do we respond to competitor Y?").
- Assemble a cross-functional team: strategy, finance, operations, sales, and customer-facing roles to reduce blind spots.

### Data Collection
- Internal data: financial statements, employee surveys, customer satisfaction scores, operational KPIs, brand tracking studies.
- External data: market research reports, competitor filings, industry publications, regulatory updates, macroeconomic forecasts.
- Conduct 5-10 stakeholder interviews to surface insights not captured in formal data.
- Review prior strategic plans and post-mortems to identify recurring themes.

### Analysis Steps
1. Brainstorm items for each quadrant independently. Aim for 8-12 items per quadrant initially.
2. Prioritize: reduce to 3-5 items per quadrant based on magnitude of impact and quality of evidence.
3. Validate each item with data. Remove anything that is opinion without supporting evidence.
4. Build the TOWS matrix: for each intersection (SO, WO, ST, WT), generate 2-3 strategic options.
5. Evaluate each TOWS strategy on feasibility, impact, and alignment with the organization's purpose.
6. Select the top 3-5 strategic options to carry forward into detailed planning.

### Presenting Findings
- Lead with the strategic question and the "so what" answer before showing the SWOT grid.
- Use an action title on every slide: "Strong brand and digital capabilities position the company to capture the underserved mid-market segment."
- Present the TOWS matrix as the bridge from analysis to action — this is where the strategic options live.
- Attach an appendix with the full evidence base for each SWOT item.

### Common Mistakes
- Listing too many items with no prioritization, which dilutes the analysis.
- Confusing internal and external factors (e.g., listing "growing market" as a strength instead of an opportunity).
- Stopping at SWOT without generating TOWS strategies — SWOT alone is descriptive, not prescriptive.
- Treating SWOT as a one-time exercise instead of updating it as conditions change.
- Failing to ground items in evidence — every item should have a data point or source attached.

## 2. Ansoff Matrix

### Preparation
- Map the company's current product portfolio and customer segments clearly.
- Identify the growth target: what revenue or market share goal is driving the need for a growth strategy?
- Understand the company's risk appetite and available resources for investment.

### Data Collection
- Current market data: market size, growth rate, share by segment, customer demographics and needs.
- Product performance data: revenue by product, margins, lifecycle stage, customer adoption rates.
- Adjacency analysis: identify markets or products that share capabilities, customers, or channels with the current business.
- Competitive landscape in each potential growth direction.

### Analysis Steps
1. Plot all current products/services against current markets on the matrix.
2. For each quadrant, brainstorm specific growth initiatives (e.g., "launch loyalty program" for penetration, "enter Southeast Asia" for market development).
3. Estimate the revenue potential and required investment for each initiative.
4. Assess risk using the benchmark success rates: Penetration ~70%, Market Development ~50%, Product Development ~40%, Diversification ~25%.
5. Build a balanced growth portfolio: most investment in lower-risk quadrants, selective bets in higher-risk ones.
6. Sequence initiatives: start with penetration (quick wins), then layer in development and diversification over time.

### Presenting Findings
- Show the current state on the matrix, then overlay the proposed growth initiatives.
- Use bubble sizes to represent revenue potential and color coding to represent risk level.
- Present a sequenced roadmap: what to pursue in Year 1 vs. Year 2-3 vs. Year 3-5.
- Quantify the growth gap: how much of the target does each initiative contribute?

### Common Mistakes
- Over-indexing on diversification because it feels exciting, despite it carrying the highest risk.
- Ignoring market penetration opportunities — often the largest, lowest-risk source of growth.
- Failing to assess whether the organization has the capabilities required for each growth direction.
- Not considering the competitive response to each growth move.

## 3. BCG Growth-Share Matrix

### Preparation
- Define the business units or product lines to be analyzed. Each should be a distinct unit with its own market dynamics.
- Agree on the relevant market definition for calculating market share (geographic scope, product category boundaries).
- Gather at least 3 years of historical data to identify trends, not just current position.

### Data Collection
- Market growth rate for each business unit's market (use industry reports or analyst estimates).
- Relative market share: the company's market share divided by the largest competitor's market share.
- Revenue and profit contribution of each business unit.
- Capital investment requirements for each unit.

### Analysis Steps
1. Calculate relative market share for each unit (your share / largest competitor's share). A value above 1.0 means you are the market leader.
2. Plot each unit on the matrix with market growth on the Y-axis and relative market share on the X-axis.
3. Use bubble sizes proportional to revenue contribution.
4. Classify each unit: Star, Cash Cow, Question Mark, or Dog.
5. Develop resource allocation recommendations: invest in Stars, harvest Cash Cows, selectively invest in Question Marks, divest Dogs.
6. Project forward: where will each unit be in 3-5 years if current trends continue?

### Presenting Findings
- Show the current-state matrix with clear quadrant labels and bubble annotations.
- Overlay a "future state" view showing where units are heading.
- Present resource allocation recommendations with specific dollar amounts or percentages.
- Include a migration strategy: how to move Question Marks toward Star status.

### Common Mistakes
- Using absolute market share instead of relative market share.
- Defining markets too broadly or too narrowly, which distorts share calculations.
- Treating the matrix as static — it should be updated annually as market dynamics shift.
- Automatically divesting all Dogs without considering strategic interdependencies.
- Ignoring the cash flow implications: Cash Cows fund the entire portfolio.

## 4. Porter's Five Forces

### Preparation
- Define the industry boundaries clearly: geographic scope, product/service category, value chain position.
- Identify the key players: competitors, suppliers, buyers, potential entrants, and substitute providers.
- Determine the time horizon: current state vs. projected state in 3-5 years.

### Data Collection
- Competitive rivalry: number of competitors, market concentration (HHI or CR4), growth rate, differentiation levels, exit barriers.
- Supplier power: number of suppliers, switching costs, input uniqueness, forward integration threat.
- Buyer power: number of buyers, purchase volume, switching costs, price sensitivity, backward integration threat.
- Threat of new entrants: capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty, regulatory barriers, access to distribution.
- Threat of substitutes: availability of alternatives, price-performance ratio, switching costs, buyer propensity to switch.

### Analysis Steps
1. Rate each force on a scale of Low / Medium / High with specific evidence.
2. Identify the 1-2 forces that most constrain industry profitability.
3. Assess how each force is likely to evolve over the next 3-5 years.
4. Determine the company's position relative to each force: is it better or worse positioned than the average competitor?
5. Generate strategic implications: how can the company mitigate the strongest forces or exploit the weakest ones?

### Presenting Findings
- Use the classic diamond diagram with the five forces arranged around "competitive rivalry" in the center.
- Color-code each force: green (favorable), yellow (moderate), red (unfavorable).
- Include a summary table with the force, current rating, trend direction, and strategic implication.
- Lead with the "so what": which forces matter most and what should the company do about them.

### Common Mistakes
- Analyzing the company instead of the industry — Five Forces is an industry-level tool.
- Treating all five forces as equally important when typically 1-2 dominate.
- Ignoring the dynamic nature of forces — they shift over time with technology, regulation, and market evolution.
- Conflating bargaining power with the quality of the relationship (a friendly supplier can still have high power).

## 5. Value Chain Analysis

### Preparation
- Map the company's end-to-end value chain from raw material sourcing to after-sales service.
- Identify the industry value chain: where does the company sit, and what activities are performed by suppliers, partners, or customers?
- Define the objective: cost reduction, differentiation improvement, or identifying outsourcing opportunities.

### Data Collection
- Activity-based costing data: what does each activity cost as a percentage of total cost?
- Benchmarking data: how do costs and performance compare to competitors or best-in-class?
- Customer research: which activities contribute most to the customer's willingness to pay?
- Process efficiency metrics: cycle times, error rates, capacity utilization.

### Analysis Steps
1. List all primary and support activities specific to the company (not generic categories).
2. For each activity, document: cost as a percentage of revenue, key performance metrics, and contribution to differentiation.
3. Benchmark each activity against competitors or industry averages where data is available.
4. Identify linkages: where does performance in one activity affect cost or quality in another?
5. Highlight sources of competitive advantage (activities where the company significantly outperforms) and improvement opportunities (activities where it lags).
6. Recommend: optimize high-cost low-differentiation activities, invest in high-differentiation activities, and consider outsourcing non-core activities.

### Presenting Findings
- Use the classic arrow-shaped value chain diagram with primary activities along the bottom and support activities stacked above.
- Color-code activities by performance: green for advantage, red for disadvantage, yellow for parity.
- Quantify the opportunity: "Reducing inbound logistics costs to industry average would save $12M annually."
- Present a prioritized action plan with quick wins and longer-term structural changes.

### Common Mistakes
- Using generic activity labels instead of company-specific descriptions.
- Focusing only on cost and ignoring differentiation contributions.
- Analyzing activities in isolation without considering linkages between them.
- Failing to benchmark against competitors, which means you cannot assess relative advantage.

## 6. Business Model Canvas

### Preparation
- Determine whose business model you are analyzing: the company, a competitor, or a proposed new venture.
- If analyzing a competitor, accept that some blocks will require estimation and inference.
- Prepare a large format (physical whiteboard or digital canvas) to capture all nine blocks simultaneously.

### Data Collection
- Customer data: segmentation studies, personas, customer journey maps, NPS scores.
- Financial data: revenue by stream, cost breakdown, unit economics, customer lifetime value.
- Operational data: key processes, partnership agreements, resource inventories.
- Market data: competitive positioning, channel effectiveness, pricing benchmarks.

### Analysis Steps
1. Start with Customer Segments: who are the most important customers? Define 2-4 distinct segments.
2. Define Value Propositions for each segment: what specific problem do you solve or need do you fulfill?
3. Map Channels and Customer Relationships: how do you reach, acquire, and retain each segment?
4. Identify Revenue Streams: how does each segment generate revenue? What pricing model applies?
5. Map the "engine" side: Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships required to deliver the value proposition.
6. Document the Cost Structure: what are the largest cost drivers? Is the model cost-driven or value-driven?
7. Assess coherence: do all nine blocks reinforce each other? Identify misalignments or gaps.
8. Compare to competitors' business models to identify differentiation or vulnerability.

### Presenting Findings
- Display all nine blocks on a single slide or page using the standard canvas layout.
- Use concise bullet points in each block (3-5 items maximum).
- Highlight the critical blocks where competitive advantage or risk resides.
- If comparing models, show side-by-side canvases for the company vs. a key competitor.

### Common Mistakes
- Being too generic in the Value Proposition block — "great customer service" is not a value proposition.
- Ignoring the relationships between blocks (e.g., a premium value proposition requires premium channels).
- Filling in blocks with what the company aspires to be, rather than what it actually is today.
- Not testing the model's internal consistency: do the revenue streams cover the cost structure?

## 7. McKinsey 7S Framework

### Preparation
- Define the organizational change or alignment question driving the analysis.
- Identify the scope: entire organization, a division, or a post-merger integration.
- Plan for extensive stakeholder engagement — the soft elements cannot be assessed through data alone.

### Data Collection
- Strategy: strategic plan documents, board presentations, competitive positioning statements.
- Structure: organization charts, reporting relationships, governance documents.
- Systems: process maps, IT architecture, performance management frameworks, planning cycles.
- Shared Values: mission/vision statements, employee survey data on culture, observed behaviors.
- Style: leadership 360-degree assessments, meeting observation, decision-making patterns.
- Staff: headcount data, capability assessments, turnover rates, engagement scores, development plans.
- Skills: competency frameworks, training records, capability gap analyses.

### Analysis Steps
1. Assess each of the seven elements independently. Document current state with evidence.
2. Define the desired state for each element (aligned with the strategic direction).
3. Build an alignment matrix: for each pair of elements, assess whether they are aligned (green), partially aligned (yellow), or misaligned (red). There are 21 unique pairs.
4. Identify the most critical misalignments — these are the sources of organizational friction.
5. Determine root causes: which elements need to change to resolve the misalignments?
6. Develop an alignment roadmap: sequence changes starting with Shared Values (the center of the model) and hard elements (easier to change), then address soft elements.

### Presenting Findings
- Use the classic 7S diagram with Shared Values at the center and the other six elements arranged around it.
- Show connecting lines between elements, color-coded by alignment status.
- Present the top 3-5 misalignments with specific evidence and recommended actions.
- Include a change roadmap with sequencing and dependencies.

### Common Mistakes
- Focusing only on hard elements (Strategy, Structure, Systems) because they are easier to assess and change.
- Underestimating the importance of Shared Values — they influence everything else.
- Treating the framework as a checklist rather than an alignment diagnostic.
- Not engaging enough stakeholders to accurately assess the soft elements.
- Recommending changes to all seven elements simultaneously, which overwhelms the organization.
